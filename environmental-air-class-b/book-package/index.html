Exam Details

The Texas Environmental Air Class B examination is one of the contractor examinations offered for the Texas Air Conditioning and Refrigeration Contractors program. The examination is administered through PSI for the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation.

  • Exam: Texas Environmental Air Class B
  • Licensing Agency: Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation
  • Testing Provider: PSI
  • Scored Questions: 100
  • Time for Scored Questions: 170 minutes
  • Non-Scored Questions: 5
  • Additional Time for Non-Scored Questions: 10 minutes
  • Passing Score: 70%
  • Exam Type: Open book

  • Endorsement: Environmental Air Conditioning

Open Book Test

The Texas Environmental Air Class B Contractor examination is an open book test.

Candidates may bring approved references identified for the examination. Open-book testing makes advance organization important because candidates must locate information efficiently while working within the testing time limit.

Approved reference books may generally be highlighted, underlined, and contain notes made before the examination. Permanent index tabs may be used when they meet testing-center requirements. Temporary tabs, removable notes, and loose additional papers are not permitted.

Candidates are not permitted to add notes, highlighting, underlining, or indexing to their books during the examination session. Preparing and organizing approved references before test day can therefore be an important part of exam preparation.

The examination includes both reference-based and knowledge-based questions. Candidates should not expect every technical question to be solved by searching a code book. Refrigeration principles, troubleshooting, airflow, controls, equipment operation, testing procedures, and system design also require practical trade knowledge.

For current Texas code preparation, candidates should become familiar with the 2024 International Mechanical Code, 2024 International Fuel Gas Code, and 2024 Uniform Mechanical Code.

Licensing Steps

Texas Air Conditioning and Refrigeration Contractor licensing is administered through TDLR. The license class and endorsement selected by the applicant determine both the permitted work and the contractor examination.

  1. Meet the age requirement. Applicants must be at least 18 years old.
  2. Meet the applicable experience requirement. The standard experience pathway requires at least 48 months of practical air-conditioning and refrigeration experience under qualifying supervision during the preceding 72 months.
  3. Use an alternate qualifying pathway when applicable. Texas provides additional experience pathways for applicants who meet specific technician-certification or other qualifying experience requirements.
  4. Select Class B Environmental Air Conditioning. The class establishes equipment-capacity limits, while the Environmental Air endorsement establishes the type of HVAC work authorized.
  5. Submit the contractor application to TDLR. TDLR reviews applicant eligibility before authorizing examination registration.
  6. Receive examination eligibility. Approved applicants receive instructions for scheduling the required examination through PSI.
  7. Prepare with the current approved references. Study Texas law, contractor rules, HVAC codes, technical references, and design manuals applicable to Environmental Air Class B.
  8. Take the Environmental Air Class B examination. Complete the scored and non-scored examination portions within the allotted testing time.
  9. Achieve the required passing score. The current passing score is 70%.
  10. Complete remaining licensing requirements. Follow TDLR procedures, including applicable insurance and licensing documentation requirements, before the contractor license is issued.

State Requirements

A Texas Class B Environmental Air Conditioning license authorizes contracting work involving environmental air-conditioning systems within the Class B capacity limits.

The Class B license permits work on individual cooling systems with a capacity of 25 tons or less and individual heating systems with an output of 1.5 million Btu per hour or less.

Environmental Air Conditioning refers to treating air for human comfort by controlling temperature, humidity, cleanliness, ventilation, and circulation. This includes common residential and qualifying commercial heating and air-conditioning applications within the Class B capacity limits.

A building or complex may contain multiple HVAC units whose combined capacity exceeds the Class B limits, provided each complete individual unit remains within the applicable Class B limitation.

Texas requires applicants to satisfy practical-experience requirements before qualifying for a contractor license. The standard pathway requires at least 48 months of practical experience during the preceding 72 months under qualifying supervision.

The contractor examination is only one part of the licensing process. Candidates must also complete the applicable TDLR application, experience documentation, insurance, and licensing requirements.

Test Information and Study Materials

Air Distribution: Air Distribution is the largest current technical category with approximately 15 questions. Study airflow, duct sizing, friction loss, velocity, static pressure, registers, grilles, return-air systems, supply systems, and practical duct design.

Equipment Requirements and Design: Approximately 12 questions involve equipment requirements and design. Review equipment capacity, load calculations, installation requirements, clearances, system selection, and proper application of residential design procedures.

Service: Approximately 10 questions address service-related knowledge. Candidates should understand common HVAC faults, system operation, refrigerant conditions, electrical troubleshooting, airflow problems, and service procedures.

Testing: Approximately 10 questions involve system testing. Review electrical measurements, refrigerant pressures and temperatures, superheat, subcooling, airflow measurement, combustion testing, and gas-system testing principles.

HVACR Systems: Approximately nine questions cover complete system operation and equipment interaction. Study heating systems, cooling systems, heat pumps, furnaces, air handlers, refrigeration circuits, and system components.

Basic Refrigeration Components: Understand compressors, evaporators, condensers, metering devices, filter-driers, accumulators, receivers, and other common refrigeration components.

Refrigeration Principles: Review pressure-temperature relationships, heat transfer, sensible heat, latent heat, refrigerant states, superheat, subcooling, and the basic refrigeration cycle.

Controls: Study thermostats, relays, contactors, pressure controls, safety controls, transformers, control voltage, and equipment sequencing.

Combustion Air: Review requirements for supplying combustion, ventilation, and dilution air to fuel-burning appliances and learn how enclosure conditions affect combustion-air requirements.

Fuel Gas and Venting: Use the International Fuel Gas Code and applicable mechanical-code provisions to review appliance venting, vent connectors, gas piping, shutoff valves, regulators, pressure testing, combustion air, and installation safety.

Manual J: Practice residential heating and cooling load calculations, including heat gain and heat loss considerations that influence system sizing.

Manual D: Review residential duct design, available static pressure, friction rate, equivalent length, airflow, fittings, and duct sizing.

Manual S: Study residential equipment-selection procedures and the relationship between calculated loads and manufacturer performance data.

Texas Laws and Rules: Become familiar with Chapter 1302 of the Texas Occupations Code and Chapter 75 of the Texas Administrative Code, including license classes, endorsements, contractor responsibilities, and regulatory requirements.

Reference Navigation: The number of books makes organization especially important. Practice identifying the type of question first so you can move directly to the mechanical code, fuel-gas code, Texas law, technical textbook, or ACCA design manual most likely to contain the answer.
